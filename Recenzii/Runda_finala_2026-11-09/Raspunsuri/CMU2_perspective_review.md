## Item 1: The paper’s central empirical programme is not specified enough to test

* Main point of criticism: This paper draws programmatic conclusions about agent-held temporal evidence while withholding the evaluation contracts needed to test the central claims, especially for the distinctive continuity-anchor levels.
* Evaluation criteria: Validity; Conclusions; Data and methodology
* Limitations status: Not mentioned in the Limitations section

#### Evidence
* Quote: "These rules become a test only through an evaluation contract for each level, fixed before any data are collected: the benchmark environment, the threat model, the competitor, the primary metric, the declared cost dimensions, the margin, the planned sample size or simulation budget, and the statistical decision procedure. No such contract is provided here."
   * Comment: This is a direct admission that the manuscript does not provide the information required to falsify any of the proposed levels. The missing elements are not minor implementation details; they include the benchmark, threat model, competitor, sample size, margin, and statistical procedure. Those are exactly the ingredients needed to decide whether agent-held evidence improves over conventional logs, monitors, or flags. Without them, the proposed programme cannot be reproduced or independently adjudicated from the paper. The issue is especially important because the paper presents a framework whose value depends on comparisons against competent conventional alternatives, not on the existence of mechanisms in isolation.
* Quote: "The other tests, including those of the continuity anchor, where the proposal is most distinctive, are offered to others."
   * Comment: This sentence shows that the component the paper itself identifies as most distinctive has not been evaluated. A perspective can propose a research agenda, but the manuscript repeatedly frames the elements of a method as already being in place and treats the programme as empirically answerable. Offering the key tests to others leaves the strongest validity question unresolved: whether raw agent-held evidence improves anything over a trusted monitor or platform-side continuity predicate. This matters because Section 5 also states that the trusted-monitor flag is expected to be equivalent or better for detection itself. As a result, the core empirical contribution is deferred rather than substantiated.
* Quote: "Data availability. No data were generated or analysed for this article."
   * Comment: This confirms that the main paper contains no new data supporting the proposed levels. The paper does cite two completed studies, but it also says one tests none of the levels and the second only tests the cheapest Level 2 candidates. Therefore the central architecture and most important empirical questions remain unsupported by direct evidence. This weakens the conclusions because the manuscript moves from a classification and protocol sketch to claims about what mechanisms could preserve evidence for agent self-models. A reader cannot determine whether the proposed benefits are real, cost-effective, or separable from platform-side competitors.
* Quote: "Over-claiming of the results, by drawing conclusions that go beyond the evidence presented (e.g. insufficient number of experiments, mismatch between hypothesis & claim)." [[1]](#ref1)
   * Comment: This methodological warning directly applies to the gap between the manuscript's broad programme and its absence of evaluation contracts. The cited reproducibility report identifies mismatch between hypothesis and evidence as a core threat to reliability in machine-learning research. Here, the hypothesis concerns persistent agents, rollback, delayed flags, revocation, and self-prediction, while the supplied evidence is mostly conceptual plus one negative Level 2 pilot. The paper's own admission that no contracts are provided makes it impossible to check whether later studies would have adequate power, baselines, or cost accounting. The claim should therefore be narrowed to a proposal for future protocol development, not presented as a method whose elements are already in place.

## Item 3: The Pilot 2 equivalence and non-inferiority language is not statistically supported by the reported sample sizes and margins

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper treats Pilot 2 scheduling and identifiability results as closing a candidate line while the supplementary report says several margins are descriptive, some equivalence claims use very few seeds, and many comparisons were uncorrected.
* Evaluation criteria: Appropriate use of statistics and treatment of uncertainties; Conclusions; Validity
* Limitations status: Mentioned in the Limitations section, but not justifiable

#### Evidence
* Quote: "A level is closed — and the corresponding part of the hypothesis abandoned for that regime — if its competitor is non-inferior on the level's primary metric within a margin fixed in advance, the smallest difference that would matter [30], and is no worse on any cost dimension declared in advance: hardware, energy, latency, storage, communication, recomputation, error rates, availability, robustness to attack, and administrative complexity."
   * Comment: The main paper sets a demanding closure rule: non-inferiority within a meaningful prespecified margin and no worse performance on declared cost dimensions. This rule is appropriate in principle, but the supplementary report does not meet it uniformly for Pilot 2. It reports some descriptive margins, limited confidence intervals, and small samples. Therefore the evidence presented for closure is weaker than the rule the paper says should govern closure. The mismatch affects conclusions because the main paper cites Pilot 2 as a negative answer for cheap Level 2 candidates.
* Quote: "Small samples. T-A 5 seeds per clock; SuperNI 3 seeds; the authors' code one pair (R1, R2) plus two crossed runs from shared task-0 adapters (stage 1), one run per cell; seed 42 throughout, as fixed in their code; one task order (order 1 of the paper)."
   * Comment: These sample sizes are too small to support broad equivalence or non-inferiority claims in stochastic neural training regimes. One run per cell in the authors' code cannot separate clock effects from run-to-run noise, hardware effects, data-order effects, or interactions with the starting adapter. The report itself notes stochastic decoding and GPU non-determinism elsewhere, making small-n inference especially fragile. A negative or equivalent result in this setting should be reported as preliminary, not as closing a line of inquiry. The limitation is too central to be cured by merely listing it after the conclusions.
* Quote: "Equivalence margins are descriptive (±2 points; ±5% on loss); only T-A and S2 use paired confidence intervals, and those with few seeds."
   * Comment: This sentence undercuts the formal status of the claimed equivalence. Descriptive margins are not the same as justified smallest effects of interest, and the report says only two analyses used paired confidence intervals. Even those analyses have very few seeds, so the intervals are likely sensitive to individual runs. If a closure rule depends on non-inferiority or equivalence, the statistical procedure must be uniformly prespecified and adequately powered. Otherwise, the correct conclusion is uncertainty within limited experiments, not closure.
* Quote: "Many comparisons without correction; verdicts follow the fixed rules, and secondary numbers are descriptive."
   * Comment: Multiple uncorrected comparisons increase the chance that some apparent exceptions or equivalences reflect noise. This matters because Pilot 2's plan changed over time and included technical rounds, audits, sensitivity checks, SuperNI runs, authors' code runs, and repeated crossed runs. Even if some tests were governed by local rules, the overall evidential narrative aggregates many comparisons. The paper should clearly distinguish confirmatory endpoints from exploratory diagnostics and should avoid using uncorrected exploratory results to support closure. The current presentation does not make that separation strong enough for the conclusions drawn.
* Quote: "The SESOI is thus independent of the outcome of the study, and should be determined before looking at the data. A SESOI should be chosen such that inferences based on it answer a meaningful question." [[2]](#ref2)
   * Comment: This external equivalence-testing guidance shows why descriptive margins are inadequate for strong closure. Pilot 2's margins need substantive justification as smallest effects that would matter for replay scheduling, retention, or cost, not just numerical thresholds. The supplementary report admits that several margins are descriptive, which means the equivalence claim may not answer a meaningful theoretical or operational question. Since the main paper's closure rule explicitly invokes the smallest difference that would matter, the Pilot 2 execution should meet that standard. Until it does, the negative conclusion should be framed as suggestive within narrow regimes.
* Quote: "As the SESOI becomes smaller, the equivalence bounds become narrower, and the sample size needed to obtain a sufficiently narrow confidence interval increases." [[2]](#ref2)
   * Comment: This is directly relevant to the small-seed Pilot 2 comparisons. If the authors want to declare equivalence within ±2 points or ±5% loss, they need enough runs to make the confidence interval sufficiently narrow under expected training variability. The supplementary report instead acknowledges few seeds and one run per cell for important checks. That is not enough to show that small differences are reliably absent, especially under stochastic decoding and fp16 non-determinism. A properly powered equivalence design is needed before abandoning the candidate line on scheduling grounds.

#### Concrete Action Item

The plan should justify each SESOI from downstream utility or computational cost, perform power or precision calculations before running, define primary and secondary endpoints, correct or hierarchically control multiple comparisons, and report TOST or non-inferiority tests with confidence intervals for every closure claim. ## Item 4: The dual-clock section contains mathematical inconsistencies in the core definitions

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper defines the experiential trajectory index and the change-rate ratio with inconsistent symbols, including a self-ratio that is identically one, which undermines the formal clarity of the dual-clock proposal.
* Evaluation criteria: Validity; Data and methodology; Clarity and context
* Limitations status: Not mentioned in the Limitations section

#### Evidence
* Quote: "The second role — modelling the agent's own adaptation — relates the time reference to an experiential trajectory index $c_{e}$, defined as accumulated change along the trajectory, $c_{a}(t) = \Sigma_{\tau = t} D(S(\tau), S(\tau + 1))$ over committed steps."
   * Comment: This sentence introduces the index as $c_e$ but then defines $c_a(t)$, which elsewhere denotes the time reference or anchor-related clock. The mismatch is not a typographical issue in a peripheral equation; it occurs at the point where the paper defines the second major role of the architecture. It makes it unclear whether accumulated change is meant to be $c_e$, $c_a$, or a relation between the two. The summation index is also unusual as written because $\Sigma_{\tau=t}$ lacks an upper bound in the extracted text. A formal proposal for agent-held evidence needs unambiguous state variables and update equations.
* Quote: "Read against the time reference, $\rho = \Delta c_{s} / \Delta c_{s}$ is the rate at which the agent changes, and the ratio of net displacement to accumulated change measures how directed that change has been."
   * Comment: As written, $\rho = \Delta c_s / \Delta c_s$ is identically one whenever the denominator is nonzero. It therefore cannot represent the rate at which the agent changes. The surrounding prose suggests the intended expression may be something like $\Delta c_e / \Delta c_s$, but the paper does not state that. This is a major clarity problem because the rate is one of the central quantities that links accumulated experiential change to non-restorable time. The review verification script demonstrates this by evaluating the written formula for several different hypothetical accumulated-change values and obtaining the same value of one each time.
* Quote: "The algorithm updates exponential moving averages of the gradient ($m_t$) and the squared gradient ($v_t$) where the hyper-parameters $\beta_1$, $\beta_2 \in [0, 1)$ control the exponential decay rates of these moving averages." [[5]](#ref5)
   * Comment: This Adam quote is an example of how optimizer-state variables are normally defined when they are used as evidence or telemetry. The variables are named, their update rule is specified, and their role in later parameter updates is explicit. By contrast, the manuscript's dual-clock section conflates $c_e$, $c_a$, and $c_s$ at the point where the accumulated-change index and change rate are defined. Because Pilot 2 and the future Level 2 tests depend on whether such indices contain information beyond optimizer telemetry, notation errors make the hypothesis harder to audit. The paper should repair the definitions before presenting the dual clock as a self-contained formal component.

#### Concrete Action Item
* Action type: Fix the writing
* Original text: "The second role — modelling the agent's own adaptation — relates the time reference to an experiential trajectory index $c_{e}$, defined as accumulated change along the trajectory, $c_{a}(t) = \Sigma_{\tau = t} D(S(\tau), S(\tau + 1))$ over committed steps. The index is non-decreasing and is maintained as one running number; a query about an interval needs only the value stored at its start."
* Suggested text: "The second role — modelling the agent's own adaptation — relates the time reference to an experiential trajectory index $c_e$, defined as accumulated change along the trajectory, $c_e(t_1)-c_e(t_0)=\sum_{\tau=t_0}^{t_1-1}D(S(\tau),S(\tau+1))$ over committed steps, with $c_e(0)=0$. The index is non-decreasing when $D$ is non-negative and is maintained as one running number; a query about an interval needs only the value stored at its start. Read against the non-restorable time reference, $\rho=\Delta c_e/\Delta c_s$ is the accumulated-change rate over the interval, while the ratio of net displacement to accumulated change measures how directed that change has been."
* Location: Section 4, paragraph beginning "The dual clock"


## Item 5: The continuity-anchor sufficiency claim is broader than the detection cases the protocol actually covers

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper overstates the continuity anchor as necessary and sufficient for distinguishing suspension, restoration, and operation even though its own discussion and standard rollback literature show unresolved fork, latest-state, and adversarial scheduling cases.
* Evaluation criteria: Validity; Conclusions; Data and methodology
* Limitations status: Not mentioned in the Limitations section

#### Evidence
* Quote: "If $N$ exceeds $N(t_{0})$, the agent has operated since that state was saved and has been restored; if $N$ equals $N(t_{0})$ while $c_{a}$ has advanced beyond its calibration error, it has been suspended; if both have advanced together, it has operated without interruption. The pair $(c_{a}, N)$ is necessary and sufficient for this distinction, and it is what state-continuity protocols already maintain for data [36, 40, 47, 61]."
   * Comment: This is a strong sufficiency statement, but it is only valid under a narrow set of assumptions about commits, live counter availability, crash consistency, and what counts as restoration. For example, restoring a checkpoint after elapsed time but before any later committed step produces the same live counter and advanced time pattern as suspension under the described classifier. A same-machine fork is also not revealed before one branch advances the shared counter. The paper later acknowledges several such cases, which means the sufficiency claim should be scoped more carefully. The review-side `continuity_anchor_counterexamples.py` script demonstrates these ambiguous classifications in a minimal state machine.
* Quote: "A fork of the same checkpoint on the same machine shares the time reference and the device-unique responses, and is revealed only when the branches contend for the same counter: once one branch commits, the other finds $N$ ahead of its own record."
   * Comment: This sentence directly limits the earlier sufficiency statement. Before branch contention, the fork is not detected by the pair even though copying or forking is one of the discontinuities the paper says temporal evidence should address. That makes detection delayed and conditional, not simply necessary and sufficient. The distinction matters operationally because a forked agent could act before the first counter contention occurs. The manuscript should not present the pair as sufficient for discontinuity detection without specifying the point in the lifecycle at which detection is guaranteed.
* Quote: "A clone moved to another device can detect the change of substrate if its state carries a commitment to the original device's unique responses [45], but the instance continuing on the original device sees no change. Neither case reveals which branch is the original or the legitimate successor; only a continuity policy outside the agent can decide that."
   * Comment: This further shows that the anchor does not by itself solve copying, originality, or legitimacy. It can sometimes detect substrate change, but the original device's continuing instance receives no evidence of the clone. The paper correctly notes that legitimacy requires an external policy, but that qualification conflicts with broad claims about the anchor distinguishing trajectory discontinuities. If the agent cannot determine which branch is legitimate, the continuity evidence is incomplete for identity and governance purposes. The sufficiency claim should be restricted to a specified local freshness predicate, not generalized to forks and copies.
* Quote: "The architecture has also its limitations. While sealing prevents a malicious OS from reading or arbitrarily modifying persistently stored enclave data, rollback attacks remain a threat. In a rollback attack a malicious OS replaces the latest sealed data with an older encrypted and authenticated version." [[3]](#ref3)
   * Comment: This external state-continuity literature supports the paper's premise that sealed state alone does not establish freshness. It also highlights that rollback protection is about adversarial replacement of latest state with older state, not merely about a simple counter/time comparison at load. The same literature models OS scheduling, restarts, and multiple instances as part of the threat. Therefore, a sufficiency claim for an agent continuity anchor needs to specify how those adversarial capabilities are excluded or handled. The manuscript sketches assumptions but then states necessity and sufficiency more broadly than the sketch supports.
* Quote: "The ability to schedule, restart and create multiple enclave instances, enables subtle attacks that we address in this paper." [[3]](#ref3)
   * Comment: This quote is important because the reviewed paper's threat model B includes a compromised host orchestrator, which is analogous to a powerful scheduler of state and execution. Multiple instances and adversarial scheduling are not edge cases; they are central to rollback and forking attacks in protected execution. The paper's protocol sketch explicitly leaves races between checking and using a reading outside it, and fork detection is delayed until contention. These exclusions mean the anchor is not sufficient under the full practical threat model without additional protocol machinery. The paper should either add that machinery or narrow the claim.

#### Concrete Action Item
* Action type: Fix the writing
* Original text: "The pair $(c_{a}, N)$ is necessary and sufficient for this distinction, and it is what state-continuity protocols already maintain for data [36, 40, 47, 61]."
* Suggested text: "Under the commit ordering, crash-consistency, and trusted-readout assumptions stated below, the pair $(c_a,N)$ is sufficient to classify a loaded state as fresh, stale after a later committed step, or elapsed-without-commit for that lineage. It is not by itself sufficient to identify the legitimate successor among forks, to detect a same-machine fork before counter contention, or to distinguish every operational meaning of suspension from rollback without an external continuity policy; those cases require the additional policy and protocol machinery described below."
* Location: Section 4, paragraph beginning "The continuity anchor"


## Item 6: The proposal does not adequately separate accumulated-history evidence from local telemetry and shortcut signals

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper proposes accumulated-change and revocation evidence as future agent-held temporal signals, but the manuscript and supplementary report show that local update statistics, optimizer telemetry, and same-cost competitors can substitute for the purported history signal in the only completed tests.
* Evaluation criteria: Validity; Conclusions; Originality and significance; Data and methodology
* Limitations status: Mentioned in the Limitations section, but not justifiable

#### Evidence
* Quote: "Local statistics can stand in for history. The source of an episode was carried by the magnitude and variability of the updates within it, and resetting the state before every episode did not reduce decodability. Any claim that an agent uses its accumulated history must pass a reset criterion, because shortcuts available in the inputs will be found [19]."
   * Comment: This is one of the manuscript's strongest cautions, but it also undermines the evidential basis for the proposed accumulated-history programme. If resetting state before every episode does not reduce decodability, the measured signal is not accumulated history in the sense required by the hypothesis. The paper says future candidates must pass a reset criterion, but it does not provide a completed test showing that any candidate does so. This leaves the accumulated-change role as a definition rather than a validated source of agent-held temporal evidence. Merely acknowledging the shortcut problem is not enough when the proposed programme depends on excluding exactly that shortcut.
* Quote: "A model-centred quantity can be the optimizer's bookkeeping. Under Adam the size of every step is set by moments the optimizer already keeps. In a language model, the accumulated norm of parameter change was reconstructed from the learning rate times the norm of the first moment with held-out $R^2$ 0.94–0.96 (0.96–0.97 with the summed learning rate added), and a functional distance on probes was reconstructed from the change of loss on the same probes."
   * Comment: This result shows that the tested experiential indices did not provide independent temporal evidence beyond ordinary training telemetry. It directly weakens the originality and significance of the cheap dual-clock candidates. If the signal can be reconstructed from the optimizer's own bookkeeping or same-cost probe-loss changes, routing it to an agent's self-model adds cost and complexity without new information. The paper correctly calls these candidates not identifiable, but it still retains the broader programme without demonstrating a candidate that survives the same audit. Future claims need a positive example that beats telemetry, not just a list of more expensive possibilities.
* Quote: "Shortcuts are decision rules that perform well on standard benchmarks but fail to transfer to more challenging testing conditions, such as real-world scenarios." [[4]](#ref4)
   * Comment: This external definition captures the methodological risk in the paper's accumulated-history tests. A decoder or agent may appear to use historical evidence while actually exploiting local update magnitudes, loss changes, or optimizer moments that correlate with the target in the benchmark. Such shortcuts can yield high apparent performance without validating the intended construct. The manuscript acknowledges this risk, but the completed studies found exactly this pattern rather than ruling it out. That makes the positive programme premature until tests show transfer after reset, episode-order randomization, and telemetry-restricted baselines.
* Quote: "We introduce a method called TracIn that computes the influence of a training example on a prediction made by the model. The idea is to trace how the loss on the test point changes during the training process whenever the training example of interest was utilized." [[6]](#ref6)
   * Comment: This training-data influence literature illustrates how demanding a real revocation or counterfactual-influence signal is. It requires tracing changes during training and relating specific examples to later predictions, not merely accumulating a scalar distance along the trajectory. The reviewed paper lists estimated counterfactual influence as a costly candidate, but it does not provide a protocol showing how an agent-held scalar would support revocation better than checkpoints, gradients, provenance, or logs. Without that protocol, the proposed evidence risks being a coarse proxy for influence rather than an actionable revocation signal. The paper should either implement such a comparison or narrow the claim to a speculative research direction.
* Quote: "In contrast with previously proposed methods, TracIn is simple to implement; all it needs is the ability to work with gradients, checkpoints, and loss functions." [[6]](#ref6)
   * Comment: This quote also highlights an alternative conventional competitor that the paper's future revocation tests must beat. If gradients, checkpoints, and loss functions are already sufficient for a practical influence estimator, an agent-held accumulated-change index must show added value under constrained logging or delayed records. The manuscript mentions complete logs and conventional competitors, but it does not specify how the costly candidates will be compared against influence methods that use ordinary training artifacts. This omission matters because revocation and provenance are among the major claimed functions of agent-held temporal evidence. A credible test must include influence-tracing baselines rather than only event counts or generic logs.

#### Concrete Action Item
* Action type: Add new implementation
* Description: Add a construct-validity experiment for accumulated-history and revocation claims that explicitly tests against local telemetry, reset controls, randomized episode order, and influence-tracing baselines.
