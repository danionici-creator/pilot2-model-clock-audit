## Item 1: The central negative conclusion is stronger than the experiment actually tests

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper overstates its main methodological conclusion by presenting the experiential-clock hypothesis as closed or answered negatively even though the planned confirmatory forgetting-prediction test was never executed and the evidence is limited to a reconstruction gate plus scheduler comparisons.
* Evaluation criteria: Quality, Significance, Clarity
* Limitations status: Not mentioned in the Limitations section

#### Evidence
* Quote: The confirmatory test was never reached: in our implementations, every candidate clock (three parameter-space distances, a functional distance, and  $\tau$  itself) was reconstructed from cheap telemetry or from same-cost competitors with held-out  $R^2 \geq 0.90$  (exceptions only under extrapolation to a schedule family left out of training), which makes the planned hypothesis unidentifiable under the protocol's own rule.
   * Comment: This sentence states that the paper did not run the test that would directly answer whether the clock predicts forgetting beyond telemetry. The result instead depends on an internal decision rule that equates high reconstruction of the clock with non-identifiability of the forgetting hypothesis. That may be a useful screening rule, but it is not empirical evidence that residual clock information lacks predictive value for forgetting. The problem is especially important because the paper’s question is framed in terms of information about forgetting and retention, not merely whether a scalar clock can be approximated. A negative conclusion about the substantive hypothesis should therefore be conditioned on the unrun H1 test rather than presented as a completed closure.
* Quote: Under the protocol's gate G2 this makes the confirmatory hypothesis H1 (c_e predicts forgetting beyond telemetry) not identifiable, so H1 was never run and the forgetting targets were never opened.
   * Comment: This sentence confirms that the paper’s decisive gate prevents the direct confirmatory analysis from being evaluated. Because the forgetting targets were never opened, the paper cannot distinguish between a clock that is reconstructible but still has useful residual predictive information and a clock that truly adds nothing. A high held-out $R^2$ for reconstructing the clock leaves up to 10% of rate variance unexplained under the authors’ own threshold, and the paper does not test whether that unexplained component matters for forgetting. This is a major methodological gap because the paper’s headline question concerns “information beyond optimizer telemetry,” not just reconstructability of a surrogate. The evidence supports a narrower conclusion: the preregistered H1 was blocked by the authors’ gate, not empirically refuted.
* Quote: Closed, in this framework. In our implementations, every candidate for c_e tested here (D1, D1_rel, D2, D3 and FOREVER's τ) is reconstructed out of sample from cheap telemetry or same-cost competitors ( $R^2 \geq 0.90$ ; the exceptions named in §5.1 all come from extrapolation to a schedule family left out of training, and those of D3 and D1_rel in F6 did not recur in T-B, where F6 itself was sampled with new seeds and one seed was left out), so the hypothesis that c_e predicts forgetting beyond telemetry is not identifiable here.
   * Comment: This conclusion is more careful than the abstract, but it still converts non-identifiability under an author-defined gate into closure of the empirical line. The phrase “so the hypothesis ... is not identifiable here” would be acceptable if it were the endpoint of the claim, but elsewhere the paper says the open question is answered negatively. Non-identifiability is not equivalent to a negative causal or predictive result, especially when the original forgetting prediction was not analyzed. The distinction matters for significance because readers may infer that clocks were shown not to predict forgetting when the relevant outcome was deliberately not opened. The paper should keep the result at the level of a failed identifiability gate unless it adds a residual or sensitivity analysis of H1.
* Quote: Researchers specify a smallest effect size of interest (for example an effect of 0.5, so for a two-sided test any value outside a range from -0.5 to 0.5) and test whether effects more extreme than this range can be rejected. If so, they can reject the presence of effects that are deemed large enough to be meaningful. [[1]](#ref1)
   * Comment: This external guidance illustrates why a negative or equivalence-style conclusion must be tied to the specific effect being tested. The paper did not test the effect central to H1, namely incremental prediction of forgetting beyond telemetry. Instead, it tested reconstruction of clock rates and then applied a gate that precluded the outcome analysis. That procedure can justify saying the planned design could not identify H1, but it does not justify rejecting meaningful predictive effects on forgetting. The citation therefore supports the criticism that the paper needs to separate “not tested due to the gate” from “shown to be absent.”

#### Concrete Action Item
* Action type: Add new implementation
* Description: Add a residual H1 sensitivity analysis that is explicitly labeled exploratory if the authors do not want to alter the preregistered confirmatory status. The analysis should compute residuals of each candidate clock after predicting it from the telemetry set used in G2, then test whether those residuals improve out-of-sample prediction of forgetting or retention over telemetry alone. It should report nested-model performance differences, uncertainty intervals over seeds and schedule families, and the sensitivity of conclusions to reconstruction thresholds such as 0.80, 0.90, 0.95, and 0.99. The review verification script `check_confirmatory_gap.py` already verifies that the current manuscript never runs H1, so the added implementation should directly close that gap rather than relying on reconstructability as a proxy.
* Key code changes: Add a script next to `Stadiul0/tb_part2_h1.py` or extend it to accept G2 residual features; add a launcher that runs telemetry-only versus telemetry-plus-residual models on the unopened forgetting endpoints; write JSON outputs with effect sizes, confidence intervals, and threshold-sensitivity tables.
* Files modified: `Stadiul0/tb_part2_h1.py`, `Stadiul0/reproduce_analyses.py`, `Stadiul0/runs/h1_residual_sensitivity.json`, `review/verification_code_gpt-5.5/check_confirmatory_gap.py`
* Run command: `python Stadiul0/tb_part2_h1.py --mode residual_sensitivity --thresholds 0.80 0.90 0.95 0.99 && python Stadiul0/reproduce_analyses.py H1-residual`


## Item 2: The scheduler comparison is too underpowered and too confounded for the claimed practical equivalence

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper treats small scheduler differences as practically equivalent even though the main original-code comparison uses one seed, one task order, stochastic decoding, and one run per crossed cell, which is insufficient to separate clock effects from run-to-run and task-order variance.
* Evaluation criteria: Quality, Significance
* Limitations status: Mentioned in the Limitations section, but not justifiable

#### Evidence
* Quote: T-A 5 seeds per clock; SuperNI 3 seeds; the authors' code one pair (R1, R2) plus two crossed runs from shared task-0 adapters (stage 1), one run per cell; seed 42 throughout, as fixed in their code; one task order (order 1 of the paper).
   * Comment: This limitation describes very small samples for precisely the experiments that are used to support scheduler equivalence. Five seeds and three seeds are already weak for estimating small differences, and the original-code analysis is weaker still because it uses only seed 42 and a single task order. Continual-learning results are known to depend on both stochastic training and task order, so one order cannot support a general scheduler conclusion. The authors’ own wording also shows there is no replication over task orders, even though the benchmark being audited includes multiple orders. Merely listing this as a limitation is not enough because the paper still uses these data to support a broad “no measurable advantage” claim.
* Quote: Pairs sharing a task-0 adapter,  $\tau - \Sigma \eta ||\hat{m}||$ : on dbpedia (the quantity of the verdict)  $D_A1 = -0.51$  and  $D_B1 = +3.29$ ; OP  $-0.67$  and  $+1.03$ ; BWT  $-0.99$  and  $+1.78$ . Verdict under the rule fixed beforehand: not reproduced robustly. The signs are opposite, so the data do not support a stable attribution of the dbpedia difference to the clock. This does not mean that the clock has no effect: with one run per cell, run-to-run noise and an interaction between clock and starting point cannot be separated.
   * Comment: This passage directly states that the experiment cannot separate run-to-run noise, clock effects, and interactions with starting adapters. That admission undercuts the stronger practical-equivalence reading because the observed signs flip and the cells are unreplicated. The final sentence is particularly important: if the clock effect cannot be separated from noise, then the experiment is inconclusive about clock effects in the authors’ code, not evidence of equivalence. The paper should not use a one-run-per-cell crossed design to make claims about scheduler substitutability beyond this exact observed run set. A robust audit would need repeated cells, additional seeds, and additional task orders.
* Quote: Evaluation noise in the authors' code: decoding is stochastic (do_sample=True, temperature 0.02, no seed).
   * Comment: Stochastic evaluation adds another source of uncontrolled variation to an already small comparison. Even low-temperature sampling can introduce variation in exact-match classification-style evaluations, particularly when the reported differences are only a few points. Because the authors do not repeat evaluation draws or fix the sampling seed, the reported task scores mix training variation with decoding variation. This makes the one-run-per-cell original-code evidence less reliable than the text implies. The issue is major because the paper uses differences within ±2 points as evidence about clock equivalence while simultaneously acknowledging uncontrolled evaluation randomness.
* Quote: Fine-tuning pretrained contextual word embed-ding models to supervised downstream tasks has become commonplace in natural language pro-cessing. This process, however, is often brittle: even with the same hyperparameter values, dis-tinct random seeds can lead to substantially differ-ent results. [[2]](#ref2)
   * Comment: This literature evidence directly applies to the language-model and classification portions of the paper. The paper’s original-code comparison is a fine-tuning-style setting with LoRA adapters, stochastic training, and stochastic decoding, so random-seed sensitivity is a serious concern. Dodge et al. show that changing only seeds can materially change results, which makes a single fixed seed inadequate for scheduler conclusions. The paper does acknowledge seed limitations, but it still interprets within-margin aggregate differences as meaningful evidence. The cited finding supports requiring repeated seeds before accepting practical equivalence or non-superiority claims.
* Quote: Deciding of whether the benchmarks give evidence that one algorithm outperforms another should not build solely on comparing average performance but account for variance. [[3]](#ref3)
   * Comment: The paper’s original-code scheduler comparison effectively relies on aggregate differences and a fixed margin with little ability to estimate variance. Bouthillier et al. emphasize that benchmark conclusions must account for multiple sources of variation, not just average scores. This is especially relevant because the paper’s cells are unreplicated and because the adapter starting point itself moves dbpedia by several points. The cited recommendation therefore contradicts the paper’s use of one-run aggregate margins as strong evidence. A variance-aware comparison would report uncertainty over seeds, task orders, and evaluation samples.
* Quote: Our primary contributions are to demonstrate that task ordering is a significant source of variance in continual learning performance that must be properly accounted for by future work. [[4]](#ref4)
   * Comment: This continual-learning literature is directly relevant because the paper evaluates a replay scheduler on one task order in the authors’ code. If task ordering is a significant source of variance, using only order 1 cannot show that the scheduler result generalizes across the benchmark. The paper itself notes the single order, but it does not adjust the strength of its scheduling conclusion enough. A clock effect could be absent or small in one order and larger in another, especially when replay timing interacts with forgetting patterns. The citation supports requiring multiple orders before presenting practical scheduler equivalence as a robust result.

#### Concrete Action Item
* Action type: Add new implementation
* Description: Add a properly replicated scheduler comparison for the original FOREVER code with at least three seeds, all task orders reported in the FOREVER paper, and repeated deterministic evaluation or multiple stochastic decoding draws per checkpoint. The design should pair runs by seed and starting adapter where possible, estimate variance components for seed, order, clock, adapter, and evaluation draw, and report confidence intervals for OP, BWT, and each task’s forgetting. Before running the expensive experiment, use the included verification script `equivalence_power_planner.py` to estimate how many paired runs are needed for a desired confidence-interval half-width based on the observed dbpedia differences.
* Key code changes: Extend `Stadiul0/forever_repro/launch_repetari.sh` to iterate over seeds and task orders; modify evaluation scripts to set generation seeds or repeat stochastic decoding; extend `Stadiul0/forever_repro/rep_verdict.py` to fit paired mixed-effects or bootstrap variance summaries across seed/order/evaluation draws.
* Files modified: `Stadiul0/forever_repro/launch_repetari.sh`, `Stadiul0/forever_repro/eval_repro.sh`, `Stadiul0/forever_repro/rep_verdict.py`, `review/verification_code_gpt-5.5/equivalence_power_planner.py`
* Run command: `python review/verification_code_gpt-5.5/equivalence_power_planner.py --target-half-width 2.0 && bash Stadiul0/forever_repro/launch_repetari.sh --seeds 42 43 44 --orders 1 2 3 --repeat-eval 5 && python Stadiul0/forever_repro/rep_verdict.py --by-seed-order --bootstrap 10000`


## Item 3: The equivalence margins are not justified as smallest meaningful effects

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper uses practical-equivalence margins for scheduler comparisons without a substantive smallest-effect justification or an a priori power analysis, making the equivalence interpretation methodologically weak.
* Evaluation criteria: Quality, Clarity
* Limitations status: Mentioned in the Limitations section, but not justifiable

#### Evidence
* Quote: SuperNI, S2 (preregistered; 3 seeds; margin  $\pm 5\%$  on test loss). Relative difference  $\tau - \Sigma \eta \| \hat{m} \|$ :  $-0.6\%$ ,  $95\%$  CI  $[-1.9\%; +0.7\%]$ : practical equivalence.
   * Comment: This passage declares practical equivalence using a ±5% test-loss margin, but the paper does not explain why ±5% is the smallest difference that would matter scientifically or practically. A preregistered margin is not automatically a justified margin; it still needs a rationale tied to the benchmark, metric, or downstream use. The sample size is also only three seeds, so the narrowness of the reported interval is difficult to interpret without a power justification and variance-component analysis. Because the claim is framed as practical equivalence, the margin choice is central rather than cosmetic. Without justification, readers cannot know whether the paper has ruled out meaningful scheduler differences or only differences larger than an arbitrary tolerance.
* Quote: Equivalence margins are descriptive (±2 points; ±5% on loss); only T-A and S2 use paired confidence intervals, and those with few seeds.
   * Comment: This limitation concedes that the margins are descriptive and that only part of the scheduler analysis uses paired intervals. That concession is too significant to remain a limitation while the main text still says the clocks are practically equivalent in several settings. Descriptive margins can be useful for exploration, but they should not support strong equivalence language unless they are justified as smallest effects of interest and adequately powered. The few-seed setting also increases the chance that confidence intervals are unstable. The paper should either downgrade the equivalence claims to exploratory observations or provide a formal margin and power rationale.
* Quote: The equivalence range should be specified in advance, and requires careful consideration of the smallest effect size of interest. [[1]](#ref1)
   * Comment: This directly identifies what is missing from the paper’s equivalence framework. The authors specify margins before some runs, but they do not show careful consideration of why ±2 points or ±5% loss is the smallest effect size of interest. In a continual-learning replay setting, a two-point OP or task-forgetting change may be practically meaningful depending on the benchmark and the method’s claimed gains. Therefore, the paper cannot rely on preregistration alone to validate the chosen equivalence bounds. The citation supports the need for a substantive margin justification before drawing equivalence conclusions.
* Quote: A well designed study has high power to detect an effect of interest, but also to reject the smallest effect size of interest. [[1]](#ref1)
   * Comment: The paper does not provide an a priori power analysis showing that the scheduler studies can reliably reject effects at the selected equivalence margins. This matters because equivalence tests are often harder to power than difference tests when margins are narrow. The authors’ own original-code evidence shows multi-point variability on dbpedia and adapter effects of several points, suggesting that the paired sample size may be insufficient for tight equivalence claims. The cited guidance indicates that power to reject the smallest meaningful effect is part of the design, not an optional afterthought. The paper should therefore report power or sensitivity calculations for each margin and metric.

#### Concrete Action Item
* Action type: Add new implementation
* Description: Add a margin-justification and power-analysis section for every practical-equivalence claim. For each metric, define the smallest effect size of interest using either the original FOREVER gains, benchmark variance, downstream acceptability, or a cost-benefit rationale. Then compute the paired sample size or detectable interval width needed to reject effects at that margin under the observed pilot variance. The included `equivalence_power_planner.py` script provides a minimal executable starting point for author-code dbpedia differences and should be extended to T-A, S2, OP, BWT, and task-level forgetting.
* Key code changes: Add a power-analysis module that reads stored paired differences from `ta_verdict.json`, `sni_verdict.json`, and `forever_repetari_verdict.json`; compute TOST-style or CI-based equivalence sensitivity curves; write a table of required paired runs for each metric and target margin.
* Files modified: `Stadiul0/ta_analysis.py`, `Stadiul0/sni_analysis.py`, `Stadiul0/forever_repro/rep_verdict.py`, `review/verification_code_gpt-5.5/equivalence_power_planner.py`
* Run command: `python review/verification_code_gpt-5.5/equivalence_power_planner.py --target-half-width 2.0 && python Stadiul0/ta_analysis.py --power && python Stadiul0/sni_analysis.py --power && python Stadiul0/forever_repro/rep_verdict.py --power`


## Item 4: The supplied archive does not make the reported analyses reproducible as claimed

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper’s reproducibility claim is undermined because the supplied preprint/code package lacks many stored result arrays required by the analysis scripts, while the paper also reports missing historical code versions and a DOI that is not yet assigned.
* Evaluation criteria: Quality, Clarity
* Limitations status: Mentioned in the Limitations section, but not justifiable

#### Evidence
* Quote: On 9 Nov 2026, from a fresh copy of the archive with the data part unpacked, all 19 reproduced the stored outputs: every log line for line, and the JSON files identical or with relative differences below 1e-15.
   * Comment: This is a strong reproducibility claim, but it is conditional on a “data part” that is not present in the supplied review package and a DOI that the paper says is still to be assigned. When the analysis reproduction script is run on the supplied code package, most checks fail because required `.npz` and `.parquet` inputs are absent. The paper’s statement may be true for the author’s full local archive, but reviewers and readers cannot verify it from the provided materials. For a paper whose conclusions rest heavily on archived logs and rerunnable analyses, that gap is substantive. The claim should either provide the complete data part at review time or explicitly state that the current package cannot reproduce the analyses.
* Quote:
```python
#   Usage, from the repository root, after unpacking the Zenodo data part into it:  python -I Stadiul0/reproduce_analyses.py
#   Optional: names of checks to run (e.g. T-A T-B); default: all.
# Every stored JSON file that an analysis rewrites is restored byte for byte afterwards, so SHA256SUMS.txt still verifies.
```
   * Comment: This code comment confirms that the reproduction script depends on an external Zenodo data part, not just the code files included with the preprint. Because the manuscript says the DOI is still to be assigned, the dependency is not independently accessible from the paper as reviewed. This matters because many of the paper’s decisive claims depend on stored run arrays rather than rerunning the full experiments. The script design itself is reasonable, but the archive packaging is incomplete for review. A reproducibility claim should distinguish between “the author verified a private/full archive” and “the submitted materials allow independent verification.”
* Quote:
```python
def endpoints(rd):
    e = np.load(rd / "evals.npz"); acc = e["acc"]; n = acc.shape[1]
    E1 = float(np.nanmean(acc[-1])); E2 = float(np.mean([acc[-1, t] - acc[t, t] for t in range(n - 1)]))
    p = np.load(rd / "evals_pre.npz"); E3 = float(np.mean([np.nanmean(p["acc"][i, :int(t)]) for i, t in enumerate(p["task"])]))
    z = np.load(rd / "steps.npz"); cols = list(z["cols"]); D = z["data"]; ph = z["phases"]
```
   * Comment: This exact analysis code shows why the missing data files are not incidental documentation but required inputs for the reported T-A results. The script cannot compute final accuracy, BWT, pre-replay accuracy, or replay triggers without `evals.npz`, `evals_pre.npz`, and `steps.npz`. In the supplied package, these files are missing for multiple key analyses, so the results cannot be independently regenerated. That prevents reviewers from checking whether the logs and JSON summaries match the raw arrays. The issue is major because the paper’s methodological contribution depends on the integrity of these computed comparisons.
* Quote: Two code versions not preserved. The synthetic-world code of the technical rounds of 4 Nov (before the corrections of 6 Nov) and the LM training script of the runs lmtech1 and lmtech2 were not kept. Their stored results, resolved configurations and (for the LM runs) the script's hash are archived, and the analyses reproduce from them; the runs themselves cannot be repeated with exactly that code.
   * Comment: The authors acknowledge that two versions of code used for reported results were not preserved. This is not merely a minor archival imperfection because the paper’s main evidence is a sequence of changed plans, gates, and rerun analyses where exact provenance matters. Stored results and hashes can help audit consistency, but they do not allow independent reruns of those specific experiments. Combined with the absent data part in the supplied package, this reduces reproducibility to trusting archived summaries for some analyses. The limitation is too central to be dismissed while presenting the work as a closure report.
* Quote: Reproducibility, that is obtaining similar results as presented in a paper or talk, using the same code and data (when available), is a necessary step to verify the reliability of research findings. [[5]](#ref5)
   * Comment: This standard from the ML reproducibility literature directly applies to the paper’s archive claim. The submitted materials do not provide the same code and data for all reported results because some historical code was not preserved and many stored arrays are absent from the review package. As a result, only a subset of the analyses can be rerun from the available files. The problem is not that every expensive GPU run must be repeated, but that the claimed lightweight reproduction from stored results is not possible without the missing data part. The citation supports treating this as a substantive quality issue rather than a cosmetic data-availability note.

#### Concrete Action Item
* Action type: Add new implementation
* Description: Add an archive-completeness test that runs automatically before any analysis reproduction and fails with a clear manifest of missing required files. The submitted archive should include the data part or a working DOI before review, and the paper should report the exact output of this completeness test. The included verification script `check_reproduction_availability.py` already demonstrates that the current supplied package reproduces only 6 of 19 checks and identifies missing `.npz` and `.parquet` files; this should be incorporated into the official archive CI.
* Key code changes: Add a manifest generator mapping each analysis to required files; modify `Stadiul0/reproduce_analyses.py` to check the manifest before running; add a CI or local command that prints the number of available checks, missing files, and unavailable historical code versions.
* Files modified: `Stadiul0/reproduce_analyses.py`, `FILES.tsv`, `SHA256SUMS.txt`, `review/verification_code_gpt-5.5/check_reproduction_availability.py`
* Run command: `python review/verification_code_gpt-5.5/check_reproduction_availability.py && python Stadiul0/reproduce_analyses.py`


## Item 5: The paper’s narrow experimental scope is inconsistent with the breadth of its stated closure

#### Claim
* Main point of criticism: This paper frames the result as closing the empirical line on “own time as accumulated internal change,” but its experiments cover only small models, LoRA adapters, short task sequences, restricted datasets, and Adam-type settings, leaving the stated significance broader than the evidence supports.
* Evaluation criteria: Significance, Quality, Clarity
* Limitations status: Mentioned in the Limitations section, but not justifiable

#### Evidence
* Quote: The line "own time as accumulated internal change" is closed in this framework as a documented negative result.
   * Comment: This sentence is the paper’s strongest significance claim and is broader than what the experiments can support. The phrase “in this framework” narrows it somewhat, but the rhetorical object being closed is an empirical line about accumulated internal change. The actual experiments cover a small number of model sizes, optimizers, and task regimes, and the confirmatory forgetting hypothesis was not run. A documented negative result within a limited protocol is valuable, but it should not be framed as closing a line unless the line is explicitly restricted to the tested candidates and settings. The current phrasing risks overstating the contribution to readers interested in larger-scale continual learning or other clock definitions.
* Quote: What is closed is the empirical line "own time = accumulated internal change" as a source of information beyond telemetry, in this framework: small models (a 0.93 M-parameter Transformer; Qwen3-0.6B with LoRA), synthetic facts, five SuperNI tasks and four classification tasks. Nothing here concerns large models, full fine-tuning or subjective experience.
   * Comment: This passage lists substantial scope restrictions that are not peripheral. The models are small relative to current language-model practice, the Qwen experiments use LoRA rather than full fine-tuning, and the task coverage is short and narrow. The sentence says “nothing here concerns large models” but the abstract and conclusion still use closure language that can easily be read more broadly. The restrictions are particularly important because update geometry and replay behavior can change under full fine-tuning, different optimizers, and longer task streams. The paper should align its title, abstract, and conclusion with this restricted empirical scope.
* Quote: Larger models with Adam-type optimizers.  $\tau$  should remain reconstructible from the optimizer's own state, since each step is set by the moments the optimizer keeps ( $\$ 2$ ). Whether one cheap scalar still suffices is open: under full fine-tuning,  $\tau$  runs over all weights and the second moments are more heterogeneous.
   * Comment: The paper itself acknowledges that larger models and full fine-tuning may behave differently, particularly because second-moment heterogeneity increases. That acknowledgement makes the stronger closure phrasing less justified. A mechanistic expectation about Adam updates is not a substitute for empirical evidence in the regimes where the paper says the question remains open. The issue is not that the authors failed to test every possible setting, but that the paper’s significance language should track what was actually tested. This quote supports downgrading the broader “closed” language to a bounded negative result.
* Quote: We model the whole benchmarking process, revealing that variance due to data sampling, parameter initialization and hyperparameter choice impact markedly the results. [[3]](#ref3)
   * Comment: This benchmark-variance evidence reinforces why narrow, single-machine, few-seed experiments should be presented cautiously. The paper’s own scope limitations involve not just dataset choice but also hardware, model scale, optimizer configuration, and code deviations. Bouthillier et al. show that such sources of variation can materially affect empirical conclusions, so extrapolating a closure claim beyond the measured regimes is unsafe. The paper does include limitations, but the headline language still implies a broader resolution of the clock idea. The citation supports restricting the significance claim to the exact experimental support.

#### Concrete Action Item
* Action type: Fix the writing
* Original text: The line "own time as accumulated internal change" is closed in this framework as a documented negative result.
* Suggested text: Within the specific Pilot 2 protocol, model scales, LoRA configurations, Adam-type optimizer settings, task sequences, and candidate clocks tested here, we found no basis to proceed from reconstructible accumulated-change clocks to a confirmatory forgetting-prediction claim, and we found no robust scheduler advantage for $\tau$ over the tested cheap clocks. This is a bounded negative result for these candidates and regimes, not a closure of accumulated internal change as a possible control signal in larger models, full fine-tuning, other optimizers, longer continual-learning streams, or untested clock definitions.
* Location: Abstract, final sentence
