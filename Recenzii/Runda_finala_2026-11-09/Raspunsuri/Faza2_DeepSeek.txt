Part A. The results of Pilot 2

A1. Do the verdicts follow from the data and the pre-fixed rules?

Mostly yes, but two verdicts are not fully supported as stated.

First, the report’s abstract says that “every candidate clock … was reconstructed … with held‑out R² ≥ 0.90 (exceptions only under extrapolation to a schedule family left out of training)” (p. 1). That is contradicted by the report’s own §5.1, which says that D1_rel and D3 “stay below 0.90 in some families even with GBDT on all telemetry (D1_rel 0.74 in F3 and 0.82 in F6; D3 0.80 in F6)” (p. 5). Those are not extrapolation exceptions; they are within the reported feature set. The report later says the earlier D3 exception was an artefact and that T‑B re‑tested it at 0.99, but the abstract does not carry that qualification. As written, the abstract overstates the reconstruction result.

Second, the report concludes that “the line ‘own time = accumulated internal change’ is closed” (p. 1, p. 2). Under the Perspective’s own closure rule, “closed” means the conventional competitor is non‑inferior on the primary metric. Here the outcome is different: the candidate was not identifiable because it was reconstructed from the competitor’s inputs. The Perspective correctly calls this “not identifiable”, not “closed” (§5, p. 11). The report’s use of “closed” is therefore inconsistent with the rule it claims to apply. The empirical result supports “not identifiable in these regimes”, not “closed”.

A2. Alternative explanations the report does not consider

For reconstruction, the report assumes that high held‑out R² means the candidate carries no information beyond telemetry. That is reasonable under the gate, but it does not exclude the possibility that the candidate carries information that is not linearly recoverable from the telemetry, or that the reconstruction model is overfitting to the schedule families used in training. The report addresses the latter with leave‑one‑family‑out tests, but the D1_rel case (0.74 in F3) suggests that the flexible model may still be learning family‑specific shortcuts. A stronger test would be to reconstruct on a family with a qualitatively different schedule shape, not just a held‑out family from the same generator.

For the scheduling comparisons, the report attributes the systematic delay of Ση‖ṁ‖ in the authors’ fp16 code to implementation differences (GradScaler, bf16 vs fp16, data stream). That is plausible but not demonstrated. An alternative is that the delay is caused by the interaction between the replay trigger and the restart of the AdamW state at each phase, which the report notes in §8.1. If the optimizer state resets at each phase, the first few steps after a restart may have artificially small moment norms, delaying the free clock relative to τ. The report does not test this.

For the repeated runs, the report concludes that the 5.3‑point dbpedia difference “cannot be attributed to the clock” because the signs were opposite in the two pairs. That is a reasonable reading, but with one run per cell the design cannot distinguish a small clock effect from run‑to‑run noise. The report acknowledges this in §5.2 and §7, but the conclusion “cannot be attributed” is stronger than “was not robustly reproduced”. A more precise statement would be that the data are insufficient to attribute the difference to the clock, not that the clock has been shown to have no effect.

A3. Are the limitations complete? Is anything claimed beyond the evidence?

The limitations section is unusually thorough for a draft report. It lists local preregistration, unpreserved code, small samples, post hoc analyses, evaluation noise, and hardware differences. Two gaps remain.

First, the report does not discuss the possibility that the reconstruction R² is inflated by the use of the same telemetry features that the optimizer uses to compute the candidate. For example, Ση‖ṁ‖ is reconstructed from η and ‖ṁ‖, which are themselves optimizer statistics. That is the point of the gate, but the report could be clearer that the reconstruction is partly tautological: the candidate is a function of the telemetry, so of course it is predictable from the telemetry. The gate is meant to test whether the candidate carries additional information, not whether it is mathematically related to the telemetry. The report should state explicitly that the high R² is expected for candidates that are algebraic functions of the telemetry, and that the gate is therefore a test of non‑trivial information, not of any information at all.

Second, the report claims that “nothing here concerns large models, full finetuning or subjective experience” (p. 2), which is appropriately modest. But the abstract’s phrase “every candidate clock” is broader than the evidence. The evidence covers three parameter‑space distances, one functional distance, and τ, in a 0.93 M‑parameter Transformer and Qwen3‑0.6B with LoRA. It does not cover other distances (e.g., representation similarity), other optimizers, or full fine‑tuning. The abstract should say “every candidate tested” rather than “every candidate clock”.

A4. Prior work

The report cites Benzing (2022) for the result that Synaptic Intelligence and MAS approximate the square root of the Fisher information, and that the bias explains SI’s performance (arXiv:2006.06357). That is correct: the abstract of Benzing’s paper says exactly that. The report uses this as a precedent for the pattern that an accumulated‑change quantity reduces to a gradient statistic. That is a fair use.

The report also cites Atanna et al. (2025) on path‑based learning‑rate schedules for Adam (arXiv:2508.05408). That paper shows that the cumulative path‑based adaptation for Adam is “conceptually inconsistent due to the optimizer’s internal preconditioning” (abstract). This is directly relevant: it is published evidence that a path‑length clock for Adam is not a clean measure of progress, because the optimizer’s preconditioning distorts the path. The report mentions Atanna et al. but does not draw out this implication. It should: Atanna et al. provide a theoretical reason why update‑norm clocks may not carry independent information under Adam, which strengthens the report’s mechanistic argument.

I am not aware of a published counterexample showing that update‑norm clocks carry information beyond optimizer telemetry under Adam. The report’s own D1_rel exception (0.74 in F3) was later re‑tested and found to be an artefact, so it is not a robust counterexample. The report’s claim that no published audit of the FOREVER clock exists is plausible, but it is a negative claim based on a Semantic Scholar check (13 citing papers on 4 Nov 2026 and 13 on 9 Nov). That is acceptable as a statement of what the author found, but it should be phrased as “we found no published audit” rather than “no published audit was found”, to avoid overclaiming.

A5. Internal consistency

There are two inconsistencies. The first is the “closed” vs “not identifiable” terminology described in A1. The second is the abstract’s claim of R² ≥ 0.90 for every candidate, which conflicts with the §5.1 table showing D1_rel and D3 below 0.90 in some families. The report resolves the D3 case later, but the abstract does not reflect the resolution. The numbers in the scheduling tables are internally consistent: the T‑A mean differences and confidence intervals match the stated equivalence margins, and the SuperNI S2 relative difference and CI match the ±5% margin. The authors’ code R1 and R2 numbers are consistent across the text and the table.

Part B. The integration in the Perspective

B1. “Not identifiable” vs “closed”

The distinction is correct. Under the Perspective’s own closure rule (§5, p. 11), a level is “closed” if the conventional competitor is non‑inferior on the primary metric. A candidate that is reconstructed from the competitor’s inputs is not tested against the competitor; it is set aside as untestable. The Perspective says this explicitly: “If a candidate is reconstructed out of sample from the competitor’s own inputs … the hypothesis is then reported as not identifiable for that candidate and regime, never as equivalence and never as support.” That is the right distinction.

The problem is that the report (document 1) uses “closed” in its conclusion, which contradicts the Perspective. The Perspective should either correct the report’s language or add a footnote explaining that “closed” in the report means “not identifiable, and therefore removed from further testing”, not “closed under the non‑inferiority rule”. As it stands, a reader moving from the report to the Perspective will see a contradiction.

B2. The amendment of the closure rule

Adding an exit for non‑identifiable candidates after the second study met that condition is post hoc. That is not automatically illegitimate: methodological rules often need clarification when a new type of outcome appears. The question is whether the amendment is principled or ad hoc.

It is principled if the rule is justified on logical grounds independent of the result. Here, the justification is strong: if a candidate is a function of the competitor’s inputs, then a test of the candidate against the competitor is not a test of the candidate’s added value; it is a test of whether the candidate can be reconstructed, which is a different question. Reporting “not identifiable” is therefore the correct outcome, not “equivalence” and not “support”. The Perspective states this justification in §5.

It would be ad hoc if the rule were added only to avoid reporting a negative result as a closure. But the Perspective does not do that: it reports the outcome as a negative result for the cheap candidates, and it leaves the costly candidates and other regimes open. The amendment is therefore legitimate, provided that the Perspective is explicit that it was added after the study and that the justification is logical, not empirical. It is explicit about the timing (“added in version 1.4, after the second study met it”), but the logical justification could be stated more sharply.

B3. Generality statements and the memory direction

The generality statements are within the evidence. The Perspective says that the second study concerns “small models trained with Adam”, and that “the index remains a definition; its cheap candidates are not identifiable in the regimes tested” (abstract). It also says that larger models, full fine‑tuning, and other optimizers remain open (§4, §6). That is appropriately cautious.

The new direction on agents that change through memory rather than weights (§4, p. 10) is also within the evidence, because it is explicitly labeled as untested: “For them, an index accumulated over the persistent state, writes and revocations included, is untested; its natural competitor is the count and size of the writes.” That is a legitimate extrapolation of the question, not a claim of result. The Perspective does not say that the memory direction has been tested; it says it is a direction for future work. That is fine.

B4. Abstract and conclusion

The abstract is accurate in substance but too long (about 420 words). It should be shortened to focus on the conceptual contribution and the key negative result. The current abstract spends too much space on the details of the second study (model sizes, R² values) and not enough on the classification of temporal evidence and the continuity anchor, which are the Perspective’s main contributions.

The conclusion says that “the statistics the optimizer already keeps proved sufficient” (p. 16). That is slightly stronger than the evidence. The study shows that the cheap candidates were reconstructed from telemetry with R² ≥ 0.90; it does not show that telemetry is sufficient in an absolute sense, only that it is sufficient to reconstruct those candidates in those regimes. The conclusion should say “were sufficient in the regimes tested” to avoid overstatement.

B5. Conceptual map vs empirical overload

The Perspective remains a conceptual map, but it is now carrying a substantial empirical annex. The second study is necessary to justify the negative result on Level 2, and the Perspective integrates it in a controlled way. However, the level of empirical detail in §4 (R² values, model sizes, seed counts) is more than a Perspective normally carries. Some of that detail could be moved to a footnote or to a companion section, leaving the main text to state the result and its implications. The balance is not yet lost, but it is close.

B6. Contradictions between the two documents

The main contradiction is the “closed” vs “not identifiable” terminology. The report says the line is “closed”; the Perspective says Level 2 is “not identifiable”. These are not the same outcome under the Perspective’s rule. The second contradiction is the report’s abstract claim of R² ≥ 0.90 for every candidate, which the Perspective does not repeat; the Perspective says “the exceptions arose only when a schedule family was left out of training”, which is consistent with the report’s later resolution but not with the report’s abstract. The two documents should be aligned before publication.

B7. Three open choices

(a) Abstract length: yes, shorten it. 420 words is too long for a Perspective abstract. A 200–250‑word abstract that states the problem, the classification, the rollback‑indistinguishability result, and the negative result on cheap candidates would be stronger and more readable.

(b) Keep the amendment of B2: yes, keep it, but frame it explicitly as a post hoc clarification justified by the logic of identifiability, not as a rule that was always there. The Perspective already says it was added after the study; it should also say why the addition is not a weakening of the standard. The key point is that “not identifiable” is a distinct outcome from “closed”, and reporting it as such is more honest than forcing it into the closure rule.

(c) Keep the Benzing precedent and the memory direction: yes, keep both. The Benzing precedent is directly relevant: it shows that a per‑parameter accumulated‑change quantity (SI) reduces to a gradient statistic (the square root of the Fisher information), which is the same pattern the report finds at the level of the whole‑model clock. The memory direction is a valuable extension because it identifies a regime where the optimizer‑telemetry argument does not apply, and it is appropriately labeled as untested. Removing it would narrow the Perspective unnecessarily.

Five highest priorities

Fix the “closed” vs “not identifiable” inconsistency between the report and the Perspective. The report’s conclusion should use the Perspective’s terminology, and the Perspective should explain why the report’s “closed” is a shorthand for “removed from testing as non‑identifiable”, not a closure under the non‑inferiority rule.

Correct the report’s abstract claim that every candidate was reconstructed with R² ≥ 0.90. The abstract must acknowledge the D1_rel and D3 exceptions reported in §5.1, even if they were later resolved, or it must present only the final resolved results.

Clarify the logical path from G2 to H1 for each candidate. The report says H1 was never run because G2 fails. But if D1_rel initially failed reconstruction in some families, G2 would have passed for D1_rel in those families. The report should explain why H1 was not run for D1_rel, or state that the initial D1_rel exception was treated as an artefact before H1 could be run.

Strengthen the prior‑work discussion by drawing out the implication of Atanna et al. (2025): path‑based adaptation for Adam is conceptually inconsistent because of preconditioning. That is published theoretical support for the report’s mechanistic argument, and it should be integrated into §2, not just cited in passing.

Shorten the Perspective’s abstract to about 200–250 words, and soften the conclusion’s “proved sufficient” to “were sufficient in the regimes tested”. This will make the Perspective more readable and more precise.
