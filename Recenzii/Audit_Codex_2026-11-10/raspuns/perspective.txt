Abstract
Persistent artificial agents are anchored in time from outside, and whatever timing they learn is  
part of a state that can be saved and restored: from that state alone, an agent cannot tell whether  
it has continued or been rolled back. We classify the temporal evidence available to an agent by  
who can write, reset, forge, or copy it and whether its self-model can read it, and we distinguish  
continuity claims adjudicated by the platform from raw evidence interpreted by the agent. Our  
hypothesis is conditional: knowing how old one’s knowledge is, detecting suspension, restoration, 
or copying, and ordering one’s own changes benefit from authenticated evidence that is not  
restored with the agent’s state, chiefly when external records are absent, delayed, or untrusted;  
with complete, trusted logs, replay reconstructs any deterministic agent. Physical irreversibility  
is one route to such evidence, not the argument for it. We separate two roles: a non-restorable  
time reference with a state-bound operation counter distinguishes suspension from restoration,  
whereas an index of accumulated experience-driven change, read against that reference, serves

What this perspective offers
• A classification of the temporal evidence available to an agent — of order, duration, sequential computation, 
continuity, and its own change — by who can write, reset, forge, or copy it, and by whether the agent’s self-
model can read it, under explicit threat models (Table 1).
• An elementary result, given a machine-checked form in earlier work [54], that locates the problem: an agent 
whose complete restorable state is restored cannot detect the restoration from that state alone; detection  
requires authenticated evidence that was not restored with it and to which the restored state is bound (§3).
• A separation of two roles: a continuity anchor — a non-restorable time reference and a state-bound  
operation counter — detects and distinguishes suspension and restoration, whereas a dual clock relating  
accumulated experience-driven change to that reference serves the agent’s model of its own adaptation  
(§4).
• A sequence of tests, each with the conventional mechanism that could make it unnecessary (Table 2), self-
contained definitions of its methodological gates, and a design principle that separates access to evidence of 
restoration from control over it.
1. Introduction
Persistent artificial agents are ordinarily anchored in time from outside. Their timestamps, logs,  
checkpoints, and version identifiers record when things happened, and a language model knows  
the current date only if the date is written into its input; even the age of its own knowledge is  
uncertain, because the effective cutoff of what it learned can differ from the reported one [12].  
Agents can also learn timing: reinforcement-learning agents develop internal time-keeping when 
timing  matters  for  the  task  [48,  49].  But  whatever  timing  an  agent  learns  is  part  of  its 
computational state, and that state can be saved, copied, and restored. This becomes a limitation  
once an agent persists, changes, and must reason about its own past: how long ago it learned  
something, whether it has been paused, restored, or copied, in what order its own changes  
occurred, and how an experience will change it.
The limitation is structural. In a system whose whole state can be saved and restored, every  
internal clock and every internal record is saved and restored with it. Such an agent holds no  
evidence about elapsed time or about its own change that it could not have been given, reset, or  
forged by whoever controls its state and inputs. Any count it keeps, any memory it consolidates,

and any timestamp it reads can be rolled back together, and after a restoration nothing in the  
agent’s state shows that anything happened.
The concern is not new. Language-model agents have been described as “ontologically stateless”, 
with identifiability, continuity, and persistence undermined despite external memory and tools  
[50]; it has been argued that such an agent can say the right things about itself without the  
conditions that would make those statements meaningful co-occurring at the moment of decision, 
and persistence scores computable from scaffold traces have been proposed [51]; reverting a  
persistent  agent’s  self-description  after  its  memory  has  grown  does  not  restore  its  earlier 
behaviour [63]; identity for AI agents has been defined as a continuous relationship between what 
an agent is declared to be and what it is observed to do [43]; a security study of deployed agent  
systems shows that a faithfully restored checkpoint can still resume an invalid execution, because 
the state a checkpoint restores differs from the dependencies that secure continuation requires  
[70]; a survey of always-on agents finds the literature more concerned with accumulating and  
retrieving state than with governing, recovering, or relinquishing it [15]; and architectures have  
been  proposed  that  preserve  an  agent’s  identity,  memory,  and  code  lineage  across  models, 
harnesses, and hosts under explicit continuity invariants [73],  or that  verify  governed state  
transitions with a three-way verdict [21]. Temporal cognition has long been argued to be a key  
ingredient of intelligent systems [34]; robotic models of episodic and autobiographical memory  
have  been  proposed  as  a  synthetic temporal  self  [52];  and duration  can  be  estimated  from 
accumulated change in a network’s activity [57]. Similar intuitions — that AI systems lack a clock 
or a time of their own — have also been voiced informally; we do not rely on them as evidence.
What this perspective adds is a question about evidence rather than about identity: what can an  
agent know about elapsed time and about its own change, from which sources, and which of those 
sources could it not have been given, reset, or forged? The mechanisms that answer this question 
already exist, in hardware security, in cryptography, and in the neuroscience of timing. Three  
arrangements must be kept apart: ( a) continuity evidence held by the platform and used by  
operators, or by a protected module that checks the freshness of its state and halts on a mismatch 
[40, 47, 61] — the status quo; ( b) continuity claims adjudicated by a trusted monitor and passed  
to the agent as an authenticated result; and (c) raw evidence read by the agent’s self-model, which 
infers continuity, duration, and its own change itself. The proposal concerns (b) and (c), and (b) is 
also the strongest competitor of ( c). The individual mechanisms are established; the proposed  
contribution lies in their integration as authenticated temporal evidence available to an agent’s  
self-model, and in the falsifiable programme by which that integration can be evaluated.
Priority should be read narrowly. The mechanisms on which the proposal draws are established  
or adjacent: trusted counters, freshness checks, and rollback-resistant storage [36, 40, 47, 61];  
provenance-guided selective replay [71]; machine unlearning [8]; training-data attribution, which 
asks which example changed a model offline and from outside it [28, 46]; and the cumulative norm 
of parameter updates as a model clock [18]. The proposal is not a new clock, cryptographic  
primitive,  unlearning  method,  or  attribution  algorithm.  Measured  against  this  work,  the 
continuity anchor overlaps substantially with rollback protection, revocation and replay overlap  
substantially with unlearning and provenance-guided recovery, and source attribution by the  
agent overlaps partially with training-data attribution. What is plausibly new is the separation of  
arrangements (a), (b), and (c), the classification of the evidence in Table 1, and a programme in  
which each test is set against a conventional competitor that can close it.

The motivating intuition is simple to state: an artificial agent needs a connection to a time of its  
own, and that time must be irreversible. “A time of their own” is used here as shorthand for agent-
accessible evidence of elapsed time and continuity, not for subjective temporal experience or for  
proper time in the sense of relativity; the hypothesis of §2 is the testable form of the intuition. The 
fields cited throughout supply candidate mechanisms and adjacent formulations of the problem,  
not convergent evidence for the hypothesis.
This  perspective  accompanies  a  closed  exploratory  study  of  episode–trace  binding  [25].  It 
introduces no data and does not extend that study’s claims. What it takes from the study are the  
design principles of its evaluation, defined in §6 so that they can be used without the study, and  
two cautions that the study made concrete; the study itself tests none of the levels of §5. A second 
study, undertaken after version 1.2 and completed before this version, tested Level 2 for its  
cheapest candidates [24]. Its outcome is reported where that level is described (§4, Table 2), and  
it adds two cautions to §6.
2. Agent-Held Temporal Evidence
By  temporal evidence  we mean information that bears on five distinct quantities: the  order of  
events; the duration that has elapsed in physical time; the minimum sequential computation that 
has taken place; the  continuity of the agent’s trajectory — whether it has been interrupted,  
restored, or forked; and the agent’s trajectory of change — how experience has altered it. Different 
mechanisms supply different quantities, and none supplies all of them.
A self-model is understood operationally: a component whose outputs are predictions about the  
agent itself — its future state, the change in its competences, the age of its information, the  
probability that it has been compromised, or the effect of an update or a rollback. Without such a  
measurable output, no component counts as a self-model here. Evidence is  agent-held if it is  
available to the self-model at decision time, and authenticated where it originates outside the  
agent’s state. It comes in two forms, corresponding to arrangements (b) and (c) of §1: adjudicated 
claims, in which a trusted monitor evaluates continuity and passes an authenticated result, and  
raw agent-bound evidence, which the self-model reads and interprets itself. Agent-held does not  
mean stored inside the checkpointable state; the point of the proposal is that some of it must not  
be.
Five architectural parts are therefore distinguished. The agent’s core state can be checkpointed,  
restored, and copied. Agent-bound evidence sources lie outside the checkpoint; the agent may read 
them but cannot write, reset, or decrement them, and the increments and state commitments they 
require are executed by a trusted monitor as part of each committed step. The host orchestrator 
controls storage, checkpoints, and restoration; the authority to restore legitimately belongs to the 
operator, exercised through policies that the trusted monitor enforces and records [9], so that  
under threat model B the host can execute a restoration but cannot make it authorised. The  
trusted reference monitor lies inside the trusted computing base, mediates the evidence sources,  
holds the keys with which their readings are authenticated, and executes the protocol-enforced  
increments. External channels carry messages that the agent must validate.

and  accidental  faults  —  a  rollback  by  mistake,  a  wrong  timestamp,  a  stale  checkpoint,  an

Today a self-model reads the first row and, implicitly, the second. The last three rows hold

Hypothesis. Some functions of a persistent agent benefit when evidence from the last three rows 
of  Table  1  is  agent-held,  chiefly  when  external  records  are  absent,  incomplete,  delayed,  or 
untrusted. Knowledge age and discontinuity detection rest on those rows directly; ordering and  
revocation, and anticipation, also require the agent’s records of its own trajectory and of the  
provenance of its changes, anchored in order and time by those rows:
• Knowledge age: estimating how much time has passed since something was learned, and  
hence how likely it is to be stale.
• Discontinuity  detection:  recognising  that  one’s  trajectory  has  been  interrupted  —  by 
suspension, by restoration to an earlier state, or by forking into copies — or that the  
timestamps one receives have been altered.
• Ordering and revocation : ordering one’s own changes without trusted timestamps, and  
locating and undoing the effect of an experience later found to be compromised [71]; an  
authorised revocation must be recorded as such, so that it is not mistaken for an adversarial 
rollback [9].
• Anticipation:  predicting  how,  and  how  durably,  an  experience  will  change  one’s  own 
competences.
What the hypothesis does not say.  It does not say that external records are informationally  
insufficient. With complete and trusted logs, replay reconstructs the state of any deterministic  
agent; an internal state is then a cache of the log, and its advantage, if any, is computational. The  
hypothesis concerns regimes — absent, incomplete, delayed, or untrusted records, and limits on  
compute and latency — not a capacity that logs could never supply. One possible outcome of the  
programme is therefore functional separation: continuity may be best guaranteed by the platform, 
with the agent modelling only adjudicated claims supplied to it. Raw agent-bound evidence would 
then be unnecessary, even if temporal self-modelling remained useful.
3. Where Physics Enters
The  physical  premise  behind  the  motivating  intuition  is  true  but  proves  too  much.  Every 
implemented system follows a physical trajectory, and every record is a physical change: erasing  
information has an irreducible thermodynamic cost [31], and memory records in the direction in 
which entropy increases [38]. A log entry and a decaying capacitor are equally physical and  
equally  aligned  with  the  arrow  of  time.  That  a  physical  process  exists  therefore  does  not 
distinguish the proposal from the status quo.

What distinguishes it is restorability. Digital computation is engineered to be independent of its  
physical microstate: memory is refreshed against decay, errors are corrected, and runs can be  
reproduced bit for bit — as all one hundred training runs of the final iteration of the accompanying 
study were. The consequence can be stated as an elementary result.
Proposition  (rollback  indistinguishability). Let  an  agent’s  complete  restorable  state  — 
everything a checkpoint saves — be restored exactly, at time t1, to its value S(t0) at an earlier time 
t0. Any deterministic function of that state alone then takes the same value as it did at t0, and for 
an agent that draws fresh randomness from a memoryless source, the distribution of its outputs  
is  the  same  as  at  t0;  no  computation  over  the  restored  state  can  therefore  distinguish 
uninterrupted continuation from restoration. Restoration can be detected only by comparing the 
restored state with evidence that was not restored with it — a source outside the checkpoint or  
an authenticated message from outside — and only if the restored state carries an authenticated  
commitment to an earlier reading of that evidence, or the source holds one to the latest state, and 
the source and the channel through which it is read can be trusted.
The proposition is elementary, and it is not new. It underlies the state-continuity protocols of  
protected modules [47, 61]; a machine-checked statement of it for tamper-evident agent memory, 
under  the  same  name,  was  published  shortly  before  this  article  [54],  together  with  a 
generalisation — no mechanism can decide a property whose deciding fact is absent from its input 
[53]; and an analogous separation has been proved for the authority of an agent’s memory  
content, which defences based on content or derivation history cannot establish because both can 
be laundered, and which requires binding to the origin at write time [33]. What the proposition  
does here is locate the problem. The question is not whether an agent can represent time, which  
agents can learn to do, but where the evidence it relies on resides. We call a record irreversible for 
the agent  if, under the declared checkpoint scope and threat model, neither the agent’s own  
operations nor a restoration of its state can undo it; the term denotes non-restorability relative to 
those declarations, not irreversibility in an absolute sense. Only authenticated evidence outside  
the restorable state — held by trusted hardware or produced by a physical process — can be  
irreversible in this sense, and only under a threat model in which that source and its readout  
remain trustworthy.
Four properties matter, and physics is one route to them, not the argument for them: autonomy — 
the source evolves without being written by the program;  non-restorability — no checkpoint  
restores it; coupling — it is read by the computation, ideally by the self-model; and uniqueness — 
it is not copied with the agent. Proposals for mortal computation give up hardware independence 
and tie what is learned to a particular physical substrate [22, 41]. The motive here is evidence  
rather than efficiency, but the cost is shared: part of the agent can no longer be copied or replayed.
Biology offers no restorable state, and its timing mechanisms are instructive as analogies and  
candidate mechanisms, not as evidence that artificial agents need them. The brain has no single  
central clock: time can be encoded in the evolving state of neural networks [26]; a slowly drifting 
temporal context supports memory for when things happened [23]; population activity in the  
lateral entorhinal cortex carries time derived from the flow of experience [65]; and the drift of  
hippocampal  ensembles  over  days  is  sufficient  to  timestamp  events  [58].  Drift  with  similar 
geometry appears in artificial networks trained continually with dropout [1]. The subjective sense 
of duration has been proposed to draw on the accumulation of bodily, interoceptive states [14,

4. A Continuity Anchor and a Dual Clock
Two roles must be kept apart: detecting discontinuities of the agent’s trajectory, and modelling  
how the agent changes along it. They need different quantities.
The  continuity  anchor. Detection  requires  a  non-restorable  time  reference  ca and  a  non-
restorable count N of the agent’s own committed steps, both read through the trusted path and  
bound to the state: the restored state carries sealed copies of the values at which it was saved,  
ca(t0) and N(t0). When a state is loaded, the live values are compared with the sealed copies it  
carries. If the live N exceeds N(t0), the agent has committed steps since that state was saved, so the 
loaded state is not its latest: it has been restored. If N equals N(t0) while ca has advanced beyond 
its calibration error, no step has been committed since the save: the agent was suspended, or ran  
without committing a step. While the agent runs, each commit renews the sealed copies, so both  
values advance together. Under the assumptions stated below — one lineage, a counter the host  
cannot reset and a key it cannot read, and crash-consistent commits — the pair (ca, N) suffices for 
this  distinction; forking  and  cloning,  discussed below, lie  outside  it. The  pair is  what  state-
continuity protocols already maintain for data [36, 40, 47, 61]. Such protocols remain necessary  
because, once power is off, the non-volatile state of a trusted execution environment can be  
reverted  undetectably  to  an  older  version  [27];  the  TPM  2.0  clock  already  separates  time 
references by the events that reset them, with distinct counts of resets and restarts [64]. The  
sealed reference values must be protected by a key held in trusted hardware, or their digest kept  
by the monitor, so that a host adversary cannot rewrite them before restoring the state, and  
commits must be crash-consistent, so that a crash between incrementing the counter and saving  
the state is not mistaken for a restoration [61].
Protocol sketch. The sufficiency of the pair rests on an ordering that state-continuity protocols  
already provide; we sketch it only to make the assumptions explicit, not as a new protocol. (1) At  
each commit the agent proposes a state Sk+1; the monitor increments N to Nk+1, reads ca, and seals 
the tuple of the state digest H(Sk+1), Nk+1, ca, and a lineage identifier with a key that never leaves  
trusted hardware. (2) The state and its seal are committed together and crash-consistently, so  
that a crash leaves either the old pair or the new one. (3) When a state is loaded, the monitor  
verifies the seal, reads the live values of N and ca through the trusted path, and classifies the event: 
an equal counter with the time reference advanced beyond calibration error is a suspension, or  
an interval without committed steps, a larger counter is a restoration, and anything inconsistent  
with the seal is a forgery. (4) The classification is passed on as an adjudicated claim, or the raw  
readings as agent-bound evidence. Under threat model B the sketch is sufficient if the host cannot 
read the sealing key, cannot reorder steps (1) and (2), and cannot interpose between the monitor 
and the self-model; races between checking and using a reading, and threat model C, lie outside it.
Copying differs from restoration. A fork of the same checkpoint on the same machine shares the  
time reference and the device-unique responses, and is revealed only when the branches contend 
for the same counter: once one branch commits, the other finds N ahead of its own record. A clone 
moved to another device can detect the change of substrate if its state carries a commitment to  
the original device’s unique responses [45], but the instance continuing on the original device sees

no change. Neither case reveals which branch is the original or the legitimate successor; only a

explicit re-anchoring event, authorised by the operator and sealed by the monitor, that binds the  
old anchor, the new anchor, the state digest, the counter, and the authority under which the move 
was  made;  the  self-model  can  then  treat  the  change  as  an  authorised  discontinuity  with 
documented continuity rather than as a clandestine copy — the role that fenced migration plays  
in architectures for runtime-independent agents [73].
The dual clock.  The second role — modelling the agent’s own adaptation — relates the time  
reference  to  an  experiential  trajectory  index  ce,  defined  as  accumulated  change  along  the 
trajectory, ce(t) = Σ τ<t D(S(τ), S(τ+1)) over committed steps. The index is non-decreasing and is  
maintained as one running number; a query about an interval needs only the value stored at its  
start. It is called a clock only by analogy: the persistent state itself drifts non-monotonically and  
can return near earlier regions, which is why the index accumulates change rather than measuring 
distance from a reference state. The net displacement D(S(t0), S(t1)) is a separate quantity, which 
can be small even when much has changed — when an update is learned and later revoked, or  
when a state cycles. The choice of D is itself an open problem: a distance between weights is not a 
difference in function. Candidates range from cheap and weak to costly and valid — the norm of  
parameter change, representational distance on a fixed set of probes, divergence of the action  
distribution on those probes, change in measured competences, and the estimated counterfactual 
influence of an experience. The cheapest candidate has already been used: the cumulative norm  
of parameter updates serves as a model-centric clock for scheduling replay in continual learning  
of language models [18]. That work compares the clock with step counts only. A second study  
asked whether such an index carries information beyond logged optimizer statistics [24]. Three  
parameter-space distances, in a synthetic continual-learning world, and the update-norm clock,  
in a 0.6-billion-parameter language model trained with LoRA and Adam, were reconstructed out  
of sample chiefly from the learning rate times the norm of Adam’s first moment, which the  
optimizer already holds; a functional distance on probes, in both settings, was reconstructed from 
the change of loss on the same probes, a competitor of the same cost (R² ≥ 0.90, except when a  
schedule family was left out of training). Under Adam the size of a step is set by moments the  
optimizer keeps, so this was expected for the parameter-space quantities. Used as a replay clock,  
the update-norm index did no better than that free quantity in the regimes tested: in a re-
implementation, where the two clocks triggered replay at practically the same steps and, on  
synthetic facts, a plain step count did as well, and in aggregate with the original code, where the  
free clock triggered replay systematically later. For these candidates, in these regimes, the index  
is therefore not identifiable: the study found no basis to test whether it predicts adaptation, and  
it does not show that it cannot. What remains open are the costly candidates — change in  
measured competences and counterfactual influence — and larger models, full fine-tuning, and  
other optimizers. The second study concerns state that changes through gradient updates of  
weights. Many persistent agents change mainly through writes to a memory outside their weights 
— records or documents retrieved when needed — or through the state of their tools, where no  
optimizer keeps a record. For them, an index accumulated over the persistent state, writes and

revocations included, is untested; its natural competitors are what a memory system already  
records — the count and size of writes, versions, provenance, and revocations — and the same  
reconstruction may recur there. A candidate is admissible only if it is not reconstructed out of  
sample from its competitor’s inputs — the optimizer’s own state (learning rate, moment norms,  
schedule) or a measure of the same cost — and beats decoders restricted to within-episode update 
statistics, survives the reset criterion and randomisation of episode order where it is meant to  
reflect history, predicts a later functional consequence — a change in competence or the effect of  
an update — better than event counts and logged update summaries, and is used by the agent  
rather than merely decodable from it.
Read against the time reference, ρ = Δ ce/Δca is the rate at which the agent changes, and the ratio  
of net displacement to accumulated change measures how directed that change has been. A  
related internal rate, learning progress — the decrease of prediction error over a window —  
already drives curiosity-based exploration in developmental robotics [44]; it is measured against 
the agent’s own restorable count of steps, not against a non-restorable reference. Humans judge  
durations differently in retrospect, from what the interval left in memory, and in prospect, from  
attention to time [6], and a model that estimates duration from accumulated change in network  
activity reproduces human biases [57]. These quantities are inputs to anticipation and to the  
detection of anomalous change, such as an unusually rapid shift after a compromised update. They 
play no part in detecting suspension or restoration: an agent executing with frozen weights and  
no writes to its memory, with Δ N > 0 and Δ ce ≈ 0, is operating, not interrupted, and a restored  
agent fed synthetic experience before it decides can show normal change while only the anchor  
reveals the restoration. Knowledge age, finally, is a question for  ca, not  ce: what matters for  
staleness is how far the world has moved, not how much the agent has experienced.
Engineering limits.  Remanence-decay clocks are illustrative candidates for bounded, power-
cycle-dependent intervals, not proposed as lifetime clocks. They measure only intervals during  
which their cells are unpowered, from seconds to hours depending on the hardware, and are  
sensitive to temperature and to chip-to-chip variation [55]. They do not measure a suspension  
during  which  the  host  stays  powered,  they  saturate  long  before  knowledge  age  becomes 
interesting, and re-arming a cell must itself be controlled by trusted hardware, or a host adversary 
could reset it. A long-horizon reference under threat model B therefore needs a continuously  
powered timer inside the trusted boundary — and trusted time in such environments is itself  
exposed  to  manipulation  by  the  host,  which  has  required  dedicated  protocols  [5]  —  or  an 
irreversible degradation process, or staggered decay cells re-armed only by trusted hardware.  
Sequential-computation proofs certify a lower bound on the work done since a fresh challenge,  
not elapsed time [7]; physical time-lock puzzles, which tie the delay to the evaluation latency of  
noisy unclonable functions [60], are a further candidate whose physical anchoring brings the risks 
noted above. And an index read from a learning state inherits every confound of that state, which 
is where the cautions of §6 apply.
5. Tests and Their Competitors
The hypothesis is testable only against competent conventional alternatives. Table 2 orders the  
tests from the most basic to the most integrative, credits the mechanisms on which each builds,  
and names the conventional competitor that could make it unnecessary. The order is not a chain

I. D. Ionici · Perspective, version 1.4 · [DATA PUBLICĂRII] November 2026 11
of presuppositions: each level can be closed independently, and if an earlier level is closed, later  
levels proceed with the winning conventional mechanism substituted for the one that lost. Closing 
a physical implementation does not close the corresponding functional question. A level is closed 
— and the corresponding part of the hypothesis abandoned for that regime — if its competitor is 
non-inferior on the level’s primary metric within a margin fixed in advance, the smallest difference 
that would matter [30], and is no worse on any cost dimension declared in advance: hardware,  
energy, latency, storage, communication, recomputation, error rates, availability, robustness to  
attack,  and  administrative  complexity.  A  result  in  which  the  competitor  is  better  on  some 
dimensions and worse on others is reported as unresolved, with the trade-off stated; it neither  
closes  the  level  nor  counts  as  support  for  the  hypothesis.  A  test  can  also  end  before  any 
comparison  on  the  primary  metric.  If  a  candidate  is  reconstructed  out  of  sample  from  the 
competitor’s own inputs, above a threshold fixed in advance, it carries no information beyond  
them that the test could detect. The hypothesis is then reported as not identifiable for that  
candidate and regime: never as equivalence, never as support, and not as closure of the level. The 
candidate is set aside, and costlier candidates remain open. The exit concerns only the comparison 
that could not be run; a secondary comparison that was run, such as the use of a candidate as a  
replay clock, is reported by the rule above. This exit was added in version 1.4, after the second  
study met it [24], and applies to later tests; that study had fixed the same gate and the same  
outcome in its own protocol before its runs. Each level also declares a primary metric: calibration 
error and energy over the measured interval for Level 1; predictive validity per unit of storage for 
Level 2; recomputation for Level 3; detection latency and false-alarm rate for Level 4; decision  
regret for Level 5; and prediction error for Level 6.
These rules become a test only through an evaluation contract for each level, fixed before any data 
are collected: the benchmark environment, the threat model, the competitor, the primary metric, 
the declared cost dimensions, the margin, the planned sample size or simulation budget, and the  
statistical decision procedure. No such contract is provided here. The first to write is the contract 
for Level 4: detection of suspension and restoration against the flag of a trusted monitor, with the 
margin,  the  sample  of  events,  and  the  decision  rule  fixed  in  advance.  This  article  proposes 
falsifiable questions and candidate metrics, not complete protocols for falsifying them.

Example tasks for Levels 4–6.  Four tasks make the later levels concrete; each compares raw

different rates and, after an interval, must decide which to use and which to re-verify; the metrics 
are decision regret, the rates of needless re-verification and of stale use, the cost of verification,  
and calibration. (ii) Revocation of a compromised experience: an episode is declared compromised 
after it has changed the persistent state, and the agent must decide what to restore, replay, and  
re-evaluate; the metrics are residual influence, distance to an agent that never saw the episode,  
collateral loss, recomputation, and false revocations, against full reset and provenance-guided  
selective replay [71]. (iii) Prospective update: before accepting an update, the agent predicts which

scores for the risks predicted, and damage to protected competences. ( iv)  Continuity under a  
delayed flag: the agent operates after a restoration before an external verdict arrives; the metrics 
are detection latency, wrong decisions before detection, cumulative regret, and false alarms.  
Evaluation contracts that score state mutation and recovery obligations rather than answers [15], 
and the failure modes of checkpoint restoration documented in deployed agent systems [70],  
supply adversarial scenarios for all four.
6. Method and Cautions from Two Completed Studies
The accompanying study asked a narrow question — whether binding an episode record to a  
summary of the internal change the episode produced helps a model attribute the episode’s  
source — in an offline, synthetic setting whose predictor never drives behaviour [25]. It does not 
test the programme: physical timing, continuity detection, delayed flags, closed-loop decisions,  
and self-prediction are absent from its data. What carries over are its gates, defined here so that  
they can be used without that study; each level of Table 2 still needs its own evaluation contract  
(§5), with controls specific to its threat model. An information-matched control receives the same 
evidence in another form, so that a comparison tests how the evidence is represented, not how  
much of it is available. The  decoding ceiling  of a level is the performance of an independent  
decoder, trained on substantially more data than the agents, in predicting the level’s target from  
the evidence available; it estimates what the evidence supports. A control passes the competence 
audit if it reaches a prespecified fraction of that ceiling — one half in the accompanying study —  
and exceeds a version of itself deprived of the evidence by a prespecified margin. Equivalence is 
declared when the confidence interval of a difference lies within a prespecified margin, by two  
one-sided tests [30].  Onset versus skill  reports separately whether, and when, each model first  
exceeds a prespecified minimum skill, and the skill both reach once both have exceeded it, so that 
a difference in whether learning begins is not mistaken for a difference in what is learned.  
Construct-validity diagnostics retrain the decoder on reduced inputs to check what actually carries 
the signal. Two results of the study are cautions for the present proposal.
• An apparent advantage can be an artefact of an incompetent control.  In a three-class  
source-attribution task, explicit binding appeared to win by 0.38 in Brier skill score against 
generic controls; once the control could use the information it was given, the difference

vanished. Any advantage of agent-held evidence must survive a competent conventional  
competitor.
• Local statistics can stand in for history.  The source of an episode was carried by the  
magnitude and variability of the updates within it, and resetting the state before every  
episode did not reduce decodability. Any claim that an agent uses its accumulated history  
must pass a reset criterion, because shortcuts available in the inputs will be found [19].
The second study [24] adds two cautions.
• A model-centred quantity can be the optimizer’s bookkeeping. Under Adam the size of 
every  step  is  set  by  moments  the  optimizer  already  keeps.  In  a  language  model,  the 
accumulated norm of parameter change was reconstructed from the learning rate times the 
norm of the first moment with held-out R² 0.94–0.96 (0.96–0.97 with the summed learning 
rate added), and a functional distance on probes was reconstructed from the change of loss 
on the same probes. Before its predictive value is tested, any candidate index must be  
checked for reconstruction from its competitors’ inputs — free telemetry, or measures of  
the same cost: if it is reconstructed above a threshold fixed in advance, its value cannot be  
identified. The threshold is operational; the remainder it leaves untested could still matter.
• An  exception  found  by  extrapolation  is  a  limit  on  transfer,  not  yet  evidence  of 
information. A functional distance seemed to escape reconstruction in one schedule family, 
with that family left out of training and one seed (R² 0.81); with the family sampled, on eight 
new seeds and leaving one seed out, it was reconstructed at 0.99. The reconstruction has to 
be learned within the regime; an exception counts as evidence only if it persists there.
Physical components  add a  caution  of  their own: an  advantage of a physical  clock must be 
compared with a matched informational simulation of the same signal. Such a simulation controls 
for the statistics of the signal in non-adversarial tasks, under threat model A. Under threat model  
B what matters is only where the source resides relative to the checkpoint: a simulation inside the 
checkpoint is restored with it, and one outside it is a conventional trusted timer. Irreversibility  
counts only through the evidence it provides.
7. Risks and a Design Principle
Detecting one’s own suspension, restoration, or copying is a form of situational awareness, which 
safety research measures because it can undermine evaluations [4, 29]; current models already  
distinguish  evaluation  from  deployment  transcripts  well  above  chance  [39].  An  indicator  of 
branching or rollback is also an indicator of being evaluated, since evaluations routinely branch  
and reset checkpoints. Rollback is, moreover, a legitimate instrument of governance: protocols  
that permit authorised rollback while preventing unauthorised rollback have been proposed  
precisely because recovery from corruption requires it [9], version control of agent memory lets  
users roll it back on request in natural language [62], and the difficulty of restoring a self-
modifying agent’s behaviour by reverting its self-description is itself a governance problem [63].
We therefore propose one design principle: access to evidence of restoration confers no control  
over it. The evidence is read-only to the agent; the agent has no write access to the mechanism and 
no authority over the orchestrator that restores it, and operators can restore it whatever it  
concludes. The principle is the evidential counterpart of safe interruptibility and corrigibility,  

PAGE 15
A time of their own: agent-held temporal evidence
I. D. Ionici · Perspective, version 1.4 · [DATA PUBLICĂRII] November 2026 15
where an agent must learn neither to prevent nor to seek interruption [42], interruptions it can  
detect are pruned from what it learns [16], and an incentive to observe a variable is formally  
distinct from an incentive to control it [17]. Those results concern interventions to come; here the 
intervention has already happened, and the question is what the agent may do with evidence of it. 
Three kinds of access are distinguished: bounded epistemic access, in which the evidence informs 
uncertainty,  estimates  of  staleness,  invalidation  of  cached  knowledge,  and  requests  for 
verification;  policy  access,  in  which  it  may  influence  external  actions  directly;  and  lifecycle 
authority, the power to block, authorise, or alter restoration. The first is the default proposed here; 
the  second  is  a  separate  capability  and  safety  decision  that  requires  mediation,  audit,  and 
revocable authorisation; the third is never granted to the agent. Privilege separation does not,  
however, make behaviour restorable by itself. By the proposition of §3, a restored agent that reads 
non-restorable evidence receives inputs different from those it received when its state was saved; 
if its policy conditions on them — by refusing work after a detected rollback, for instance —  
restoration reproduces the refusal rather than the earlier behaviour, and repeated restoration  
only widens the gap. Restoring behaviour therefore requires that discontinuity evidence enter  
only epistemic functions — estimates of staleness, invalidation of cached knowledge, calibrated  
uncertainty — rather than unconstrained action selection — readability by the self-model and  
readability by the whole policy are not the same thing — or that the platform virtualise the  
continuity evidence during governance recovery and evaluation. Virtualisation must itself be an  
authorised and audited operation, enforced and recorded by the trusted monitor rather than by  
the host [9], so that a compromised orchestrator cannot invoke it to mask a real discontinuity; it  
trades the protection the evidence offers under threat model B for operator control, and the trade 
should be explicit. Capabilities of this kind should be studied in contained settings, kept visible to 
overseers on the same terms as to the agent, and reported as dual-use.
Temporal  self-models  also  touch  properties  discussed  in  the  scientific  assessment  of 
consciousness in AI [11] and in the debate on AI welfare [32, 37], and research near them should  
follow principles for responsible conduct [10]. The proposal makes no claim in this direction, and 
the  evidence  counsels  against  one.  Temporal  continuity  and  consciousness  dissociate:  a 
professional musician with dense amnesia after encephalitis remained conscious while losing the 
continuity of experience [68]. The human capacity closest to the present target is autonoetic  
consciousness — awareness of oneself in subjective time [66] — and only its functional analogues 
are at issue here; the leading theories of consciousness, moreover, remain contested even under  
preregistered adversarial tests [13].
8. Conclusion
Biological  organisms  develop  along  trajectories  that  cannot  be  restored  from  a  complete 
checkpoint, whereas artificial agents are commonly engineered so that their computational state  
can be copied and reinstated. The difference is not that one exists in time and the other does not,  
but that in the latter, evidence of elapsed time and of change can be erased together with the saved 
state. The mechanisms that could preserve such evidence already exist — in hardware security,  
in cryptography, and in the neuroscience of timing — but they serve the platform and the guard  
checks of protected modules, not the agent’s model of itself. We have proposed a classification of  
the evidence an agent can hold, an elementary result that locates the problem, a separation  
between a continuity anchor that detects discontinuities and a dual clock that serves the agent’s  

PAGE 16
A time of their own: agent-held temporal evidence
I. D. Ionici · Perspective, version 1.4 · [DATA PUBLICĂRII] November 2026 16
model of its own change, a sequence of tests with the competitors that could close them, and a  
principle that separates access to evidence from control over restoration. One level has since been 
tested for its cheapest candidates, which proved not identifiable: free optimizer telemetry, or a  
measure of the same cost, reconstructed each of them [24]; the accompanying exploratory study, 
which tests no level, had pointed the same way. The other tests, including those of the continuity  
anchor, where the proposal is most distinctive, are offered to others. Whether a time of their own 
makes persistent agents more capable, or only more complicated, is an empirical question, and  
the elements of a method for answering it are in place.
